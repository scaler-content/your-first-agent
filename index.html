1.0

Why a plain LLM isn't enough

Large language models are brilliant, but on their own they are stuck inside what they learned during training. This section is about the two ways we break them out, without retraining the model.

A such as Claude learns by reading a huge amount of text, once, during . After that its knowledge is frozen. That creates four practical problems for anyone building a real product.

✦Think of it like this

Picture the class topper who has read every book in the library, and is then locked in an exam hall. No phone, no newspaper, no access to your company's files. Ask about last night's IPL match and they can only guess. Ask them to book you a Tatkal ticket on IRCTC and the best they can do is explain how you would book it.

RAG is slipping the right pages under the door. An agent is giving them a phone, a laptop and permission to make calls on your behalf.

Two fixes that don't need retraining

You could or retrain the model on new data, but that is slow, costly, and goes stale again the moment your data changes. Two lighter approaches solve most problems instead:

RRetrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)

Before the model answers, fetch the most relevant pieces of your data and put them in the prompt. The model now answers from facts it can see. It knows more.

AAI agents

Give the model a goal, a set of tools, and a loop in which it can reason, call a tool, look at the result and decide what to do next. It can do things.

From "knowledge retriever" to "actor"

Many teams first build a RAG chatbot that answers questions about their own data. It works, and then users immediately ask for more:

  • "Don't just tell me the leave policy. Apply leave for Friday."
  • "Find a free slot with the design team and book the meeting."
  • "Update the ticket and tell the customer it's fixed."

Moving from a passive system that retrieves knowledge to an active one that takes action is exactly where most developers get stuck. This section gives you the blueprint for that move. Keep two questions in mind as you read:

  1. What is the actual difference between a chatbot and an agent?
  2. How do we go beyond clever prompts to systems that carry out tasks reliably?

The usual path looks like the journey at the top of this page: a basic LLM, then single prompts, then chatbots, then chatbots grounded with RAG, and finally agents that reason, use tools and act.

1.1

RAG: an open book for the model

Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) lets a model answer from information it was never trained on, by finding the right text and handing it over just before the model answers.

RAG combines an LLM with an information retrieval system. The name tells you the three moves, in order:

  1. R

    Retrieve

    Search an external (a database, an API, or a library of documents) for information related to the user's question.

  2. A

    Augment

    Add that information (the context) to the original question, building one detailed .

  3. G

    Generate

    The LLM reads the prompt and writes an answer that is factual and relevant, because the facts are right there in front of it.

✦Think of it like this

RAG turns a closed-book exam into an open-book exam. Before you answer, a helpful librarian runs to the shelves, brings back the three most relevant pages and places them on your desk. You still write the answer yourself, but now you are reading from the page instead of guessing from memory.

The full RAG pipeline

The three moves above hide some important engineering. A complete RAG system has two halves: work you do once to prepare your documents, and work that happens every time someone asks a question.

Step 1. Loading (ingesting) the data

Load the documents you want people to ask about: PDFs, wiki pages, web pages, support tickets, rows from a database. This step is called . Garbage in, garbage out: if a document is outdated here, every answer built on it will be outdated too.

Step 2. Chunking

Split each document into smaller pieces of text, called . Two reasons:

  • The model can only read so much at once. Every LLM has a , a maximum amount of text it can take in one go. You cannot paste a 2,000-page manual into one prompt.
  • Long inputs bury details. Models tend to pay most attention to the start and end of a long input and skim the middle. This is called the . In a long story, the model might miss a character who appears halfway through. Small, focused chunks avoid that.
✦Think of it like this

Chunking is turning a thick textbook into a stack of revision flashcards. Before an exam you don't reread the whole book; you pull out the three cards that matter for this question.

Try it

Change the chunk size

Too small and a chunk loses meaning ("…carry forward to the next…"). Too big and you are back to one long block. Most teams start around a few hundred words, often with a little overlap between neighbouring chunks, and tune from there.

Step 3. Embedding

Each chunk is turned into an : a long list of numbers (a ) that captures what the text means. An embedding model does this. On AWS a common choice is Amazon Titan Text Embeddings v2, the same model we use for agent memory in Section 3 (amazon.titan-embed-text-v2:0).

The magic property: texts with similar meaning get vectors that sit close together, even if they share no words. "How many days off do I get?" lands near "Every employee gets 24 days of paid leave".

✦Think of it like this

An embedding is a GPS coordinate for meaning. Koramangala and HSR Layout have different names but nearby coordinates. In the same way, "salary credited late" and "payroll delay" are different words with nearby vectors.

Try it

See meaning as distance

Real embeddings have around a thousand dimensions. We have squashed some sentences down to two so you can see them. Pick a question and watch where it lands and which neighbours it pulls in.

Step 4. Storage in a vector database

Each chunk is saved together with its vector in a (or vector store). These systems are built for one job: given a vector, quickly find the stored vectors closest to it. That is called . Common options:

OptionWhat it isA good fit when
Amazon OpenSearch ServiceManaged search and analytics service on AWS with vector searchYou want a managed AWS service that scales to large collections
PineconeFully managed, hosted vector databaseYou want a dedicated vector service with nothing to operate
FAISSOpen-source similarity-search library that runs inside your appExperiments, notebooks and smaller, in-memory indexes
ChromaLightweight open-source vector databaseQuick local development and prototypes
PostgreSQL + pgvectorAn extension that adds vector columns to PostgresYou already run Postgres (for example Amazon RDS or Aurora)

There is no single best choice. It depends on how much data you have, how fast answers must be, and what your architecture already uses.

✦Think of it like this

A normal library shelves books alphabetically. A vector database is a library that shelves books by meaning: walk to the "monsoon" aisle and you find books on rain, flooding and umbrellas side by side, whatever their titles.

Step 5. Retrieval

When a user asks a question, the system embeds the question with the same embedding model, then asks the vector database for the closest chunks. It returns the results, where k is simply how many you want: the top 3, top 5 or top 10. These chunks become the supporting context for the model.

Step 6. Filtering and reranking (optional)

Similarity search is fast but rough. takes the retrieved chunks and scores them again with a more careful (and slower) model, and filtering drops chunks that are irrelevant or not allowed (old versions, another department's data). Both improve answer quality, but they add cost, latency and moving parts, so they are optional.

✦Think of it like this

Hiring at scale: a quick resume screen shortlists 50 candidates (retrieval), then a proper interview picks the best 3 (reranking). You only pay for interviews when the quick screen isn't good enough.

Step 7. Answer generation

The best chunks are passed to the language model together with the original question. The model writes a clear, relevant answer based on that retrieved information, ideally pointing to which chunk each fact came from.

1.2

What is an AI agent?

An agent is someone, or something, that does a task on your behalf. An AI agent uses an LLM to reason, plan and carry out tasks for people or for other systems.

are autonomous software systems. Their tasks can be as different as compiling research, paying bills, planning a trip or managing enterprise applications. They make decisions, adapt to new information and take action without needing a human to spell out every step. Many of them use RAG as part of their work to fetch the facts they need.

What really makes agents powerful is that they think iteratively: they try something, look at the result, adjust their approach, and keep going until the goal is reached.

?A chatbot

Like the enquiry counter at a railway station. Ask "When does the Shatabdi leave?" and you get an answer. You still have to queue, book, and check the PNR yourself.

AAn agent

Like a personal travel assistant. You say "get me to Chennai by Friday morning". They check trains and flights, book the best option, and message you if it's delayed.

An agent as a smart travel planner

A user asks: "What should I pack for New York in summer?" A plain chatbot would give generic advice. Watch what an agent does instead: it gathers facts from different places, combines them with what it knows about this user, then decides.

Agent capabilities: plan, act, reason

RAG made LLMs better at answering by adding a retrieval step. Agents build on that foundation and go much further: they make decisions and take actions on their own, even for complex, multi-step tasks. They do it with three abilities, used in a loop:

  1. 1

    Planning

    Break a high-level objective into tasks that can actually be executed.

  2. 2

    Acting

    Use tools and information (such as knowledge bases) to carry out those tasks.

  3. 3

    Reasoning

    Look at the results of each action and update the plan, again and again, until the goal is achieved.

For example, an agent could fully automate scheduling a meeting: checking calendars, sending invites, booking a room, following up when someone declines, and updating the details. That is a job you would normally give a human executive assistant. Step through it below and notice the moment the plan has to change.

      1.3

      The AWS agentic stack

      So far we have talked theory. Now we ground it in real services. AWS offers agent-building services at three levels of effort, and picking the right level is half of building a good agent.

      Think of the AWS agentic AI landscape as a spectrum. At one end are ready-made apps for business users. In the middle are managed platforms for developers who want flexibility without running servers. At the other end are highly customisable services and specialised hardware for builders who need deep control.

      ✦Think of it like this

      Dinner, three ways. Order on Swiggy: ready to eat, no effort, little choice (agentic applications). A meal kit: ingredients prepped and delivered, you cook it your way (managed solutions). Cook from scratch: buy the produce, own the kitchen, control every spice (do it yourself). This course cooks with the meal kit.

      Do it yourself

      The bottom tier gives maximum control. It provides the foundational infrastructure and tools to build, deploy and scale agentic apps from scratch, for experienced developers, ML engineers and researchers who want a say in everything from hardware to custom model architectures. Amazon SageMaker AI is the end-to-end service here: build, train and deploy machine-learning models at scale, including custom model development.

      ✦Think of Bedrock like UPI

      UPI lets any app talk to any bank through one standard. Bedrock lets your code talk to models from many companies through one API. Switching from Claude Sonnet to Claude Haiku is changing one string, which is exactly what the BEDROCK_MODEL_ID variable does in our code.

      iCustomising the brain

      You can adapt an LLM to your domain in Amazon SageMaker AI (for example, by fine-tuning on your legal or medical documents) and then make it available in Bedrock through Custom Model Import. That domain-adapted model can then act as the reasoning engine, the "brain", of your agent.

      Bedrock or SageMaker AI?

      Most agent builders live in Bedrock and reach for SageMaker AI only when they need deeper control. Here is the side-by-side view.

      ServiceBest suited forStrengths
      Amazon BedrockRapid development of generative AI apps and agentsManaged, serverless-style access to foundation models; one unified API; agent features; models from many providers
      Amazon SageMaker AIAdvanced customisation, model training, fine-tuning and specialised deploymentDeep control over training, hosting, tuning and deployment; many endpoint and infrastructure options

      In this course we use Amazon Bedrock to build and deploy agents, because it is a managed, API-first way to drive agent workflows with foundation models. SageMaker AI plays a supporting role for teams that need more: the agent's brain might be fine-tuned in SageMaker AI, or hosted on a SageMaker AI when a team wants tighter control over cost, latency or infrastructure. Together they cover everything from quick agent prototypes to heavily customised model deployments.

      ✦Think of it like this

      Bedrock is booking an Uber: you pay per trip (per ), pick the car type, and never think about servicing. A SageMaker AI endpoint is leasing your own car: you choose exactly which car, keep it ready all day, and pay for it even while it's parked, in exchange for full control.

      How an agent is built on AWS

      Before we zoom into frameworks, here is the whole build in one picture: where you write code, what the code talks to, and how it reaches users.

      1. 1

        Write it in an IDE

        A developer builds and configures the agentic app in an such as Visual Studio Code, Amazon SageMaker Studio, or an agentic IDE such as Kiro.

      2. 2

        Use a framework

        The app uses a such as LangChain or the Strands Agents SDK to define the agent's logic, orchestrate its workflow, and connect it to models and tools.

      3. 3

        Call a model, the brain

        An agent needs a reasoning engine to understand requests and decide what to do next, so the running app makes calls to model providers: Amazon Bedrock, or models hosted on Amazon SageMaker AI.

      4. 4

        Reach tools, memory and other agents

        At the same time, the app talks to tools, APIs, memory systems and other agents, over or , to fetch information, perform actions or collaborate.

      5. 5

        Show the result

        The output reaches the end user through a client application or user interface, where they interact with the agent.

      ✦STDIO vs HTTP(S), in one line each

      STDIO is talking to a colleague sitting next to you: the tool runs on the same machine, and the two programs pass messages through standard input and output. HTTP(S) is phoning another office: the tool runs somewhere else and you reach it over the network.

      The same blueprint, inside our first script

      Stage 2. LLM + RAG

      Here the LLM is in external knowledge through RAG. Instead of relying only on what it learned in training, the system first retrieves relevant information from enterprise documents, knowledge bases or databases, and passes it to the model as context. Answers become more accurate, more current and more specific to your domain.

      Example: a chatbot that answers employees' questions using your internal HR policy documents.

      Traditional RAG vs agentic RAG

      You'll hear both terms. The difference is who decides when and how to retrieve.

      Traditional RAG follows the same fixed path every time: user question → retrieve documents → send context to the LLM → generate answer. Ask "What is our company's leave policy?" and it fetches HR policy chunks and answers.

      Agentic RAG treats retrieval as one tool among many inside an agent: question → agent reasons → maybe call the RAG tool → maybe call other tools → answer or act. The agent can decide when to retrieve, refine a search, use several sources, combine retrieval with APIs, and act on what it finds. The agent is the decision-maker; RAG is one capability it uses when needed.

          Stage 3. Augmented LLM

          Now the LLM is connected to external tools and data sources. This is the shift from a passive LLM to an active one.

          The LLM is still the central brain, but on its own it is limited by its training cutoff and cannot touch the world. The augmented LLM adds three pillars. An effective agent knows where to find information (retrieval), how to use it to act (tools), and how to keep track of progress (memory). Understanding how these three talk to the LLM is the key to building reliable workflows on AWS.

          Example: an LLM that searches the internet for live information before answering, or one that writes an email and then calls an email API to send it.

          ⌘In our code: 01_hello_agent.ipynb, steps 4 and 5

          When you give the agent the current_time tool and the prebuilt calculator, you have built an augmented LLM. The model can now get the real time and do exact arithmetic instead of guessing.

          Stage 4. Agentic workflow, and stage 5. Autonomous agent

          Agentic workflow. The LLM is no longer just answering; it becomes an agent that plans, reasons and executes a sequence of steps towards a goal. It breaks a complex task into sub-tasks, decides which tool each needs, runs them, and combines the results. Planning, memory and reflection usually come along.

          Example: an agent booking a flight first checks availability through a flight API, compares prices, asks you about your preferences, and finally calls a booking API to complete the reservation.

          Autonomous agent. A more advanced form of the agentic workflow, with more independence and persistence. It learns from its environment, adapts its plans and pursues long-term objectives without constant human input, using feedback loops and self-correction.

          Example of an autonomous agent: one that continuously monitors market trends, researches new investment opportunities, and executes trades on its own based on predefined strategies and risk limits.

          ✦Agentic workflow

          A wedding planner with a checklist: venue, caterer, decorator, done. When the wedding is over, the job ends.

          ✦Autonomous agent

          A self-driving car: it never stops perceiving the road, deciding, steering and correcting until the trip ends, and learns from every drive.

          !Autonomy needs limits

          An agent that trades money without asking anyone must have hard limits: maximum trade size, allowed instruments, a kill switch, and a full log of every decision. This is the red arrow from the stage diagram, made real.

          Stage 6. Multi-agent collaboration

          The most complex stage: several autonomous agents working together towards a shared goal. Each has a specialised role, its own knowledge or tools, and they communicate and coordinate to solve problems too big for one agent. It mimics how a human team works.

          1.6

          Agent protocols: MCP and A2A

          Big agent systems run into a plumbing problem: every agent needs to talk to many tools, and often to other agents. Protocols are the shared plugs and sockets that make this manageable.

          An agent often has to call external tools, APIs or databases to fetch information or take actions it can't do alone. A travel agent asked to book a flight may need a flight API, the customer's profile and a payment service. As tools and workflows multiply, teams need one consistent way to connect agents to external systems. That is the problem solved by the Model Context Protocol (MCP), which standardises agent-to-tool communication.

          Large systems also tend to use several specialised agents instead of one do-everything agent. A planner agent hands work to a booking agent, which in turn coordinates with a compliance or payment agent. Now the challenge is also agent-to-agent communication, which is what the Agent-to-Agent (A2A) protocol standardises.

          Both exist so that agents and tools from different teams and vendors can work together without custom glue code for every pair. A is just an agreed set of rules for that conversation.

          ✦Think of it like this

          Remember the drawer full of different phone chargers? USB-C fixed it: one socket shape, any device. Or think of UPI: before it, each payment app needed a deal with each bank; now any UPI app works with any bank. MCP is that idea for agents and tools, and A2A is that idea for agents and other agents.

          Model Context Protocol (MCP)

          MCP gives agents a standard way to discover and use tools when there are many of them. Its promise is write once, use anywhere: build an MCP server for your database once, and any MCP-aware app or agent can use it.

          MCP was introduced by Anthropic in late 2024 as an open protocol, and is now governed by the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation. It provides a standard way to connect AI models to virtually any data source or tool. It uses a design: you expose data and actions through lightweight MCP servers, and AI apps connect to them as MCP clients. Four parts make it work:

          PartWhat it isUSB-C version

          Agent-to-Agent protocol (A2A)

          A2A is an open standard for agents to discover each other and work together. Google introduced it in 2025; it is now also hosted by the Agentic AI Foundation, alongside MCP. It gives agents a common language even when they were built with different frameworks by different companies.

          Why do we need it? Imagine building a trip planner. You need a hotel agent, an activity agent, a flight agent and a local guide agent, all coordinating. Building every one yourself is a lot of work. You would rather reuse agents that others have already built, but you have no idea how they are implemented inside. A2A gives you a standard way to collaborate with them anyway.

          Through A2A, the travel agent acts as the for the whole trip. It delegates specific tasks to specialised agents without understanding their internal code. Each agent, whoever built it and however, communicates using standard A2A messages. This decentralised approach means you don't rebuild every component, and good specialised agents get reused.

          ✦Think of it like this

          A wedding planner doesn't cook, decorate or play music. They hire a caterer, a decorator and a DJ, agree on what's needed in a standard way (date, guests, budget, menu), and coordinate. They never need the caterer's recipes. A2A is that standard way of hiring and coordinating, for agents.

          How A2A works

          There are four players: the user, a client agent (Agent A, which acts for the user), and one or more remote agents (Agent B, the server, which does the work). The client agent sends tasks to remote agents over A2A and collects their results.

          Agents advertise their capabilities with an , a small document usually published at /.well-known/agent-card.json. The client agent reads cards to find the best agent for a task, then uses A2A to talk to it. Here is roughly what the card of the flights agent you build in Section 5 looks like:

          Strands Agents and the agent loop

          You now know the parts of an agent and the protocols around it. To actually build one, you need a framework that wires those parts together. In this course that framework is Strands Agents.

          An agent framework gives you the building blocks: a way to define and orchestrate tools, store memory, and configure the LLM. As we saw in 1.4, popular frameworks include LangChain (with LangGraph) and Strands. We use Strands to build our "Hello World" agent, and here's why it feels so simple.

          Strands Agents is an open-source from AWS that takes a model-driven approach to building and running agents with very little code. You describe the goal and hand over the tools; the LLM (for example, one on Amazon Bedrock) plans, reasons and chooses tools by itself.

          ✦Think of it like this

          Workflow-driven is giving a new driver turn-by-turn directions: if a road is closed, they're stuck. Model-driven is telling an experienced cab driver "Kempegowda airport, fastest route": they pick the roads, check traffic and reroute on their own.

          Inside Strands: the agent loop

          This is the most important picture in the section. Every Strands agent, from the ten-line hello world to a production system, runs this loop. It's also called the or agentic workflow: take a high-level prompt, use a model to reason, use tools to act, and deliver a result.

          Walking through the loop with a story prompt

          Say the user's prompt is "Write a short story about a robot who wants to be a painter." That's the goal. Here is what happens, step by step.

          1. 1

            Invoke the agent

            The prompt is sent to the agent, the central orchestrator. Its job is to understand the prompt, plan how to fulfil it, and manage the conversation with the model and the tools.

          2. 2

            Enter the agentic loop

            Everything inside the dashed box is the loop, where the thinking and acting repeat.

          3. 3

            Invoke the model

            The agent sends the prompt (or a refined version, plus the conversation so far) to the model, the LLM.

          4. 4

            Get a response

            The model generates text based on its input.

          5. 5

            Reasoning

            The model understands the prompt, breaks it down and decides the next step. It might reason: "To write a story I need a character, a setting, a plot and an ending. I might want to look up some art ideas."

          6. 6

            Tool selection

            The model decides whether it needs an external tool. If the prompt had asked for an illustration too, it might choose an image generation API.

          7. 7

            Execute the tool

            If the model asks for a tool (to find information, make an image, do a calculation), the agent runs it. Tools are the external APIs or functions the agent has access to: image generation, text-to-speech, a search engine. For a plain story the model may need no tool; for "…and include an image of the robot's first painting" the agent would call the image API here.

          8. 8

            Return the result

            The tool does its job and sends its result back to the agent.

          9. 9

            Go round again

            With the tool result (or straight away, if no tool was needed) the agent calls the model again with the updated context. Ask the model, the model reasons and maybe picks a tool, the agent runs it, the result goes back in. This repeats until the prompt is fully handled.

          10. 10

            Return the final response

            When the work is done, the agent collects everything, including the story written by the model.

          11. 11

            Result

            The agent hands the result back to the user: the complete short story about the robot who wants to be a painter.

            Every model response comes with a that tells the agent what to do next. tool_use means "I need a tool, run it and come back to me". end_turn means "I'm done, here's my answer". That single field is what keeps the loop going or ends it.

            iStrands in one sentence

            Strands has three core ingredients, a model, tools and a prompt, plus the agentic feedback loop that lets the agent adapt to changing situations, handle the unexpected, and improve its result with every pass round the loop. Now let's build one.

            Step 4. Give the agent a tool

            A tool is just a Python function with three things: the @tool , so the model knows what arguments to send, and a so the model knows when to use it.

            01_hello_agent.ipynb, Step 4
            from datetime import datetime
from zoneinfo import ZoneInfo

from strands import tool


@tool
def current_time(timezone: str = "Asia/Kolkata") -> str:
    """Get the current date and time in a given IANA timezone.

    Args:
        timezone: IANA timezone name, for example "Asia/Kolkata" or "America/New_York".
    """
    return datetime.now(ZoneInfo(timezone)).strftime("%A, %d %B %Y %I:%M %p %Z")


agent = Agent(model=model, tools=[current_time])
agent("What time is it right now in Bangalore and in New York?")

            How each part of the function is used

            1. @

              @tool

              Registers the function as a tool the agent can offer to the model.

            2. :

              timezone: str = "Asia/Kolkata"

              Becomes the input schema: one text argument, optional, with a default.

            3. "

              The docstring

              Becomes the description. The model reads it to decide whether this tool fits the question.

            Roughly what the model is shown

            { "name": "current_time", "description": "Get the current date and time in a given IANA timezone.", "inputSchema": {"json": { "type": "object", "properties": { "timezone": { "type": "string", "description": "IANA timezone name, for example \"Asia/Kolkata\" or \"America/New_York\"." } }, "required": [] }} }
            ✦Think of it like this

            The docstring is the description on a restaurant menu. The waiter (the model) can't taste the food in the kitchen (your code); it recommends a dish only from what the menu says. Vague menu, wrong dish. That's why good docstrings matter so much in Section 2.

            The same idea in LangGraph

            Strands isn't the only way. hello_langgraph_agent.py builds the same kind of agent with LangChain's create_agent, which runs on the LangGraph runtime and replaces the older langgraph.prebuilt.create_react_agent. It builds a agent: reason, act with a tool, observe, repeat. The same loop you met in 1.7, just with different wording.

            section-01-your-first-agent/hello_langgraph_agent.py
            """Section 1: Hello World agent with LangGraph / LangChain on Amazon Bedrock.

`create_agent` (from langchain.agents) is the current way to build a
ReAct-style agent that runs on the LangGraph runtime. It replaces the older
`langgraph.prebuilt.create_react_agent`.

Run:
    python hello_langgraph_agent.py
"""

import os

from langchain.agents import create_agent
from langchain.chat_models import init_chat_model
from langchain.tools import tool

MODEL_ID = os.getenv("BEDROCK_MODEL_ID", "global.anthropic.claude-sonnet-4-6")
REGION = os.getenv("AWS_REGION", os.getenv("AWS_DEFAULT_REGION", "us-east-1"))


# A simple tool the agent can call
@tool
def greet(name: str) -> str:
    """Greet someone by name."""
    return f"Hello, {name}! Welcome to the world of AI agents."


# Initialize the LLM via the Bedrock Converse API
llm = init_chat_model(MODEL_ID, model_provider="bedrock_converse", region_name=REGION)

# Create a ReAct agent with the tool
agent = create_agent(model=llm, tools=[greet], system_prompt="You are a friendly assistant.")

# Run the agent
response = agent.invoke({"messages": [{"role": "user", "content": "Please greet Alice and Bob."}]})

# Print every message in the conversation: user -> AI (tool calls) -> tool results -> AI answer
for message in response["messages"]:
    print(f"{message.type}: {message.text}")

            ✦Think of it like this

            Strands is an automatic car: get in and drive. LangChain is a box of Lego: every part you could want, you assemble them. LangGraph is designing a metro map: you draw every station and track, so you control exactly where each train can go, including loops and stops where a human gets on.

            You now have the foundation every later section builds on.

            We traced how agentic intelligence grows, from a plain LLM to teams of agents. RAG gives a model fresh, private knowledge by retrieving the right chunks before it answers. Agents go further: they plan, act with tools, reason about results, and loop until the job is done. We looked at the protocols that let agents plug into tools (MCP) and into each other (A2A), and at why they complement each other.

            We mapped the AWS agentic stack and saw why Amazon Bedrock (with AgentCore) and Amazon SageMaker AI cover everything from quick prototypes to heavily customised models. Then we moved from theory to practice with the Strands Agents SDK, its model-driven agent loop, and your own Hello World agent: one line to create it, a decorator to give it a tool, a prebuilt calculator, and a system prompt to shape it.
